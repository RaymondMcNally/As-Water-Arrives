One Irish mile from Lady Erin

The old borough of Sligo ran one Irish mile (2,048 m) out from the town centre. This model takes that circle, centred on the 1798 Lady Erin monument on Market Street, and prints it at 1:2500 in 52 white tiles held together by magnets.

Its ground comes from a 2006 survey of the town and the OPW's 2011 lidar. The 11,914 buildings come from Tailte Éireann footprints and heights; roads and water from OpenStreetMap. A projector above the table lays live data over it.

The water scenarios

"Water arrives" spreads water over the ground from the sea or from Lough Gill and shows everything below the chosen level that it can reach. It is a simple illustration, not a flood forecast: it ignores tides, defences, drainage and how long a flood lasts.

The OPW flood maps

"Sea-level rise" shows the OPW's national coastal flood maps (2021): today's sea, then the sea about 2100 at 0.5, 1.0, 1.5 or 2.0 m higher, with a storm tide (a high tide plus a storm surge) that comes once in 2, 20 or 100 years on average. Even the 1-in-2-year level is a storm, not an everyday tide. The river maps also have mid-range and high-end futures, with more water in the Garavogue.

"OPW flood maps" shows the Office of Public Works' river flood extents for Sligo from floodinfo.ie: the land with a 10%, 5% or 1% chance of flooding from the river in any one year. They are indicative maps; floodinfo.ie has the official versions and their conditions of use.

Architectural heritage

"Architectural heritage" lights the 331 buildings in the National Inventory of Architectural Heritage inside the circle, 17 of them of national importance. Many stand on the quays and along the river: Sligo Abbey already touches today's river flood zone, and with the sea 1 m higher the 1-in-100-year flood reaches the Town Hall, the Yeats Memorial Building and Harbour Mills.

Electricity

"Electricity" shows the overhead lines by voltage, the four ESB 38 kV substations, and the local substations, transformers, poles and solar panels, from OpenStreetMap and Tailte Éireann mapping. Underground cables are not public. A red ring marks equipment inside today's 1-in-100-year flood zones: when a substation floods, the power cut reaches well beyond the water.

Shoreline of the docks

"Shoreline of the docks" shows the land won from the sea between the Ballast Quay and the end of the Deepwater Quay: about 35 ha that was sea or mudflat at high tide. The Ballast Wall had enclosed 4.4 ha before the first Ordnance Survey of 1837; the railway causeway to the Deepwater Berths added 2.1 ha by 1910; and the Upper Flat behind it, still mud on the 1940 map, was filled from about 1958, much of it as the town's landfill until 1994 (28.3 ha). The lines are the high-water mark of 1837 (dashed), 1910 and 2023. The 1837 and 1910 lines were traced from the Ordnance Survey sheets and are good to about 15 m.

Protected nature sites

The river through the town is itself protected: the Garavogue is part of Lough Gill Special Area of Conservation, and the harbour opens into Sligo Bay, a Special Area of Conservation and a Special Protection Area for birds. Building near them must be screened for its effect on the site. Boundaries from the National Parks and Wildlife Service.

Planning permissions

"Planning permissions" plays Sligo County Council's decisions inside the circle year by year since 2017, when its planning went online, coloured by type. A permission is not a building: some are never built and some lapse. The number of homes is read from each application's description, so it is approximate.
